Help verify the record

These transcripts are produced by machine, and the machine is wrong in specific, findable ways. This page lists where a person’s judgment would help most. You do not need to be technical, and you do not need to fix anything — telling us that a line is already right is as useful as telling us it is wrong.

How to submit

On any transcript page, right-click a line (or use the “Correct or verify this line” button) and a form opens with that line pre-filled. Edit it and submit, or submit it unchanged to confirm it is correct. Every accepted change becomes a public commit, so a correction is exactly as visible as the original text.

Roll calls — who voted, and how

the highest-value gap

Speaker attribution is about 97% accurate on ordinary speech and only 53–66% on short turns. Roll-call answers are short turns, which means the most consequential thing in a meeting — who voted which way — is what the pipeline is worst at. Summaries deliberately omit vote tallies for this reason.

If you are watching a roll call and the transcript attributes a “here”, “yes” or “no” to the wrong person, that correction is worth more than almost anything else on this page.

Measured across the archive
Names called in a roll call19,872
With a response captured in the audio17,838
With no response recorded at all2,034

Those 2,034 are not a transcription error and we cannot fix them by re-processing: the clerk reads a name, the microphone was not on, and nothing was recorded. The vote happened and the audio has no trace of it. If you know how a particular vote went — from the posted minutes, from being in the room — that is information the recording does not contain.

Who is speaking

14 labels look unreliable

Speakers are matched across meetings by voice. For a few labels the match is demonstrably unstable — the same name is attached to recordings that do not sound like each other, which usually means one label is covering more than one person. If you recognise a voice and the name above it is wrong, say so.

Median consistency across the archive is 0.81, so these sit well below everything else. The likely cause is mechanical: whoever reads the roll has other people’s one-word answers folded into their voice profile.

Surnames are a known weak point. Speakers were first identified by surname alone and the switch to full names was never finished, so 650 labels are still just a surname. For 49 of those the surname belongs to more than one person in the archive — the corpus holds both a Richard and a Carol Caraviello, nine different Murphys and seven McLaughlins — so these cannot be merged automatically without risking putting one person’s words under another’s name.

Meeting dates

2 unresolved

A mistyped date in a video title moves a meeting to the wrong day and stops it being matched with its agenda. Eleven were corrected in September 2026 using four checks: a missing agenda, the wrong weekday, which councillors were present, and the resolution numbers cited. These two resist all four.

  • City Council — titled 1/7/25

    Uploaded 7 January 2026, a year after the date it claims, and no council meeting took place that day. The council met 6 January 2026. Two other January meetings were titled “–25” and proved to be 2026, so this is probably a third. Not yet transcribed, so we cannot check who was in the room.

  • City Council — titled 08/01/25

    A Friday, and City Council has met on a Tuesday 92–100% of the time every year since 2011. It is also only 195 seconds long, so it may not be a meeting recording at all.

The same meeting posted twice

35 to spot-check

Meetings are often published by several outlets, and about 75 duplicate pages were removed in September 2026 by comparing transcripts rather than titles. What remains is mostly a full meeting sitting alongside short clips of it, which is fine. A real duplicate looks like two recordings of similar length on the same date. If you spot one, the shorter copy is usually the one to drop — unless it is longer, in which case it may contain part of the meeting the other is missing.

One known example: the Charter Study Committee on 15 July 2024 has two copies at 68 and 57 minutes.

What the archive cannot tell you

Stated plainly, because it matters for how much weight to put on any single page:

  • Vote tallies are not summarised. Short-turn attribution is too weak to publish who voted which way. Outcomes appear only when stated plainly on the record (“the motion passes”).
  • Minutes are mostly not published. The city posts an agenda for 95% of meetings and minutes for 24% — 19% in 2024, falling to 9% in 2026. For most recent meetings the transcript is the only account of what happened.
  • Summaries are machine-written and cover about 5% of meetings so far. Every one is marked as such.
  • Speaker names are a best guess unless someone has confirmed them. About 13,300 were assigned by hand; roughly 7,700 by automatic matching.
Figures measured 28 September 2026 across 2,326 transcripts. This archive is unofficial and is not affiliated with the City of Medford or Medford Public Schools; the recordings belong to their publishers. If a source is missing, or something here is wrong, the correction form above reaches a person.
